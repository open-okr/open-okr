# Ways of working, mapped onto the product

Four shapes of organisation, and what each one should put in a space, a cycle
and an initiative. Pick the row closest to yours, set it up that way, and
change it when it stops fitting.

This page is a guide, not a rule. Nothing here is enforced by the product, and
none of it is a threshold. The rules the product does enforce are in
[the numbers](numbers.md).

## The three containers, in one paragraph each

**A space is a group of people who hold each other to account.** It is the unit
a weekly session is held for, the unit access is granted on, and the unit the
alignment score is read per. If two groups never sit in the same room to talk
about progress, they are two spaces.

**A cycle is a period with a start, an end and a close.** Objectives belong to
one. A cycle is what makes scoring possible, because a score is a comparison
between what was committed at the start and what is true at the end. One
workspace can run several cycles at once when different parts of it move at
different speeds.

**An initiative is a body of work that moves a measure.** It is not a key
result and it is not a task. A key result says what changes; an initiative says
what you are doing about it; tasks are how the initiative gets done.

## Four common shapes

| Your shape | Spaces | Cycles | Initiatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| A startup, one team, under about 15 people | One space for the whole company | One quarterly cycle | One per bet, usually two to four live at a time |
| A company of functions: product, sales, marketing, operations | One per function, plus a company space above them | One quarterly cycle for everyone, so the company can close together | One per function per objective, owned by the function |
| Product teams with their own missions | One per team, each with its own KPI tree | One quarterly cycle, but team objectives often span two | One per experiment or release theme |
| A matrix: functions and cross-functional programmes | One per function, plus one per programme | One quarterly cycle, and an annual cycle above it for the programmes | Cross-functional ones live in the programme space, not in a function |

### A startup

One space, one cycle, everything in it. Do not create a space per person or a
space per project; both produce sessions nobody attends. The signal that you
have outgrown this is a weekly session where half the room has nothing to say.

### Functions

The company space holds the company objectives. Each function space holds
objectives that contribute to them, which is what the alignment score reads: a
function objective that restates the company objective scores badly on purpose.

Run one cycle across every space so the close happens once. A function on its
own cycle cannot be read against the company's.

### Product teams

Give each team its own space and its own KPI tree. A product team's health is
usually a small number of measures it watches continuously, and those are KPIs,
not key results: a KPI has a corridor and no end date, a key result has a
baseline, a target and a cycle.

### A matrix

The trap is putting a cross-functional programme's objectives in whichever
function happens to lead it. They then compete with that function's own work
for attention in its session. Give the programme a space, put the people in it,
and let it hold its own weekly session.

## Where things commonly go wrong

**A space per project.** Projects end. Spaces are meant to outlive a cycle,
because the point of one is a standing group with a standing session. Use an
initiative for a project.

**A cycle per team.** Every team gets its own quarter, offset by a few weeks,
and the company can never close. Use one cycle and let teams differ in what
they commit to inside it.

**Initiatives written as key results.** "Ship the new onboarding flow" is an
initiative. The key result is what shipping it is supposed to change. If you
cannot say what it changes, the initiative may not be worth doing.

**Objectives with no space.** A workspace-level objective nobody owns in a
space has no session where it is discussed, so it is never checked in on and
goes stale. The product will show it as outdated, which is correct and is not
the fix.

## Starting templates

The onboarding wizard offers three, and each one is one of the shapes above.
Skipping the step and starting empty is a first-class answer; the templates are
a faster way to a first quarter, not a better one.

| Template | The shape it assumes |
|---|---|
| OKR starter cycle | A team running OKRs for the first time: one objective, its measures, a KPI, and this week's session |
| Company onboarding | A company setting its first quarter: three company objectives across customers, product and operations |
| Product team space | A product team inside a wider workspace: its own space, two team objectives, and a KPI tree of its own |

Everything a template writes is ordinary content you can edit or delete. A
template applies once; a workspace that already holds goals is left alone.
